\noindent A separate experiment asked whether an English--Chinese image writer could learn from translations it had already produced incorrectly. Two writers saw the same 39,008 ordinary bilingual pairs and 352 screened comparisons. One also received a loss on the relative scores of the two complete answers. Both were tested on repeated corrections, on other rehearsed passages and on four unseen books.\par

\section*{Correcting an emitted glyph sequence}
The editor reads the pictured source and a rough visual translation, then generates glyphs or copies them from the draft.\cite{pointer} Both arms rehearsed the same targets. One also learned to score the entire reference above a previously emitted error, using mean log probability per glyph. Two bilingual graders screened the errors. An individual recheck reduced 400 proposed comparisons to 352, with some omissions still accepted.

\section*{Testing transfer}
The preference writer earned 65/96 credits on corrections, 8/96 on ordinary rehearsed passages and 1/96 on fresh books. Matched likelihood earned 61, seven and zero. The text comparator earned 92/96 book credits, supplied references 71 and unrelated foils zero. The preference writer preserved both polarities in 197/236 controlled pairs, against 225 before further training. A separate CPU run reproduced all 24 sampled output images and stops.
\section*{Where the writer loses meaning}
On the books, every neural writer assigned its free output a higher mean glyph probability than the reference in all 96 cases. With the correct prefix provided, related bilingual passages supplied the right next cell on 187 of 1,274 parent errors, versus 54 for random passages.
