\paragraph{Writing a phrase at a known site.}
The previous comparison established that classical glyph order influenced the choice between two supplied modern phrases. We next gave the writer an insertion boundary and the preceding modern images and asked it to draw the missing phrase with a learned ending. The source-conditioned fit reproduced 337/5,376 discovery phrases and 268/5,413 replication phrases exactly, compared with 157 and 150 for the matched fit trained with the source withheld (Fig.~\ref{fig:visual-phrase}). The chapter-bootstrap gains were 2.70--4.05 and 1.68--2.70 percentage points (95\% intervals). The original editor recovered 220 and 197 at the same sites. A local frequency rule recovered 868 and 808, a deficit for the new writer of about ten percentage points on each cohort. It recovered 20/5,213 multi-glyph phrases and none of 3,281 whose complete insertion type was absent from training. It solved both members of four of 293 phrase exchanges independently. When scoring both offered assignments, it chose the recorded assignment 219 times, falling to 163 after reversing the source and mirroring the boundary.

Wrong first glyphs accounted for 9,516 of the 10,184 unsuccessful completions. In a separate physical check, the frozen renderer reproduced 10,783 of the 10,789 reference phrases from their correct measured ink. On 170 sites the free writer produced the entire reference followed by extra ink. These observations locate different errors at the first content decision and at the end of a correct phrase. The comparison uses one recorded translation and an alignment-derived site; independent translation quality remains unresolved.

We evaluated the probabilities of the recorded and emitted first-glyph measurements at each incorrect first image. The conditioned network gave its own emitted glyph the higher probability on 4,676/4,708 and 4,777/4,808 errors. The median log-probability advantage of the emitted glyph was 4.81 and 4.77 nats. Perfect maximization of the same first-glyph objective could therefore recover at most 63 of these 9,516 recorded glyphs. It could still choose other high-probability glyphs or improve a sequence-level criterion. Among cases where local counts alone solved the phrase and the writer missed the first glyph, the recorded glyph won only eight comparisons out of 1,207.
