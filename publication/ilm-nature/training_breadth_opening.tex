\noindent\textbf{Can a model learn translation by reading and writing sequences of glyph images on a single consumer GPU? We tested whether increasing the diversity of bilingual supervision improves complete answers. Two independent 1.82-million-parameter visual editors each received 11.30 million supervised glyph decisions. One saw 147,456 distinct natural sentence pairs; the other saw 24,576 pairs six times. On 96 requests from four unseen books, the broader fit conveyed six complete meanings, the repeated fit four and their starting writer five. The broader fit retained both polarities on 226 of 236 controlled pairs. The paired intervals across books include zero. The pictured source influenced the next glyph on most passages, while complete translations of unfamiliar prose remained rare.}\par
\begin{multicols}{2}
\section*{Reading and writing ink}
The writer receives aligned $32\times32$ printed cells and a rough translation computed from the same input by bilingual phrase tables. It learns to emit a glyph or copy it from the draft, then feeds back the selected shape. The learned stop sets the output length. The motor uses a finite repertoire of printed forms. Both fits used the same initial weights, supervised cell counts and optimizer steps on one RTX 4090 D.
\end{multicols}

\section*{Comparing complete meanings}
The broader fit earned one English and five Chinese credits. Quantized text Qwen3-8B earned 88/96 in the same two-order automated review, supplied translations 53 and unrelated foils zero. The gain over repeated training was 2.08 percentage points (paired 95\% interval across four works, 0.00--4.17); the gain over the starting writer was 1.04 points (0.00--3.13).
