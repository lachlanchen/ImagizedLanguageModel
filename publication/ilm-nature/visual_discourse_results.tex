\subsection*{Writing without candidate answers}
We removed the candidate names and asked an image model to write each answer one physical glyph at a time. The same writer continued English and Chinese books and answered pictured instructions. On held English fiction, the cost of predicting the next glyph fell from 5.66 to 2.64 bits per glyph after fitting. Swapping each prompt with a different English passage raised the cost to 2.83. Chinese fiction showed the same direction (10.68, 7.40 and 7.67). Supplied target history makes this an easier test than writing the rest of the passage. In free generation the writer reproduced the opening eight reference glyphs on none of 152 book windows. In the first English instruction it echoed the question about energy conservation and repeated misspelled words. The first Chinese response named alliteration but failed to produce the requested phrase (Fig.~\ref{fig:visual-discourse}a,b).

We compared predicting each pictured reply independently with a paired objective that penalized the weaker interpretation under a change in the passage. The two writers shared initialization and every fitting draw. On 1,015 reserved WinoGrande contrast pairs, both complete answers were written correctly on 60 pairs with independent training and 62 with paired training. On 237 official validation pairs the counts were 15 and 12. Neither writer succeeded on both members of any of 120 external Winograd pairs. Scoring two supplied answers by likelihood gave 13 complete external pairs for the paired writer.

A second experiment let the writer resume copying at the next source position after it had printed a glyph. We trained the source selection and free emission functions on human questions and complete answers with ordinary writing interleaved. The base writer gave none of 384 normalized reference answers. Independent source selection gave 12 and persistent selection gave 11 (Fig.~\ref{fig:visual-discourse}c). The persistent writer named tennis when asked in traditional Chinese which sport uses a hollow rubber ball and a net. All three of its correct English dialogue replies were negative yes--no answers. Erasing the current question left six correct answers across the three sources. Neither fitted system answered both different questions correctly on any of 192 passages. At the first held English book prefix the persistent writer printed \emph{no} and stopped. Carrying a source position did not solve the observed failure to condition writing on the question.
