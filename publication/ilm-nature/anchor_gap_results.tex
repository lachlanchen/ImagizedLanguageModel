\paragraph{Writing between preserved source glyphs.}
Can a writer retain a name while changing several other parts of a passage? In 55,872 paired pictured passages, 787,478 source and target glyphs have uniquely determined correspondences among the longest order-preserving bitmap alignments. We trained a source reader to select these positions and a second recurrent component to write the intervening images. Across 512 passages from 390 unseen chapters, it recovered 8,512 of the 10,085 uniquely aligned source positions and selected another 3,735. In one independent answer it conveyed the refusal of a neighbouring state. In another it preserved the ruler of Jin as speaker and conveyed his judgment, with a doubled copula (Fig.~\ref{fig:anchor-gap}).

Only these two answers received complete-meaning credit among 24 passages. The existing phrase composer received three. Keeping every source position with the same neural writer yielded zero, as did supplying the reference-derived source positions. The candidate reached its 192-image limit on 63 of 512 inputs. Supplying the intended positions raised this count to 147. The learned policy required 30,884 glyph edits to recover the recorded translations, compared with 18,419 for the composer and 19,080 for literal copying. Accurate glyph reproduction and partial recovery of copy positions leave the words between positions underdetermined.

We then replaced recurrent gap generation with a fitted distribution over finite image sequences, retaining the learned planner. On the same cohort this reduced edits to 18,873. Its two credited answers described a set of bridges and three technical pairings. Neither writer surpassed the composer. Reference translations themselves received 19 of 24 credits when read with the method identities concealed. Two included passages addressed a different subject altogether. A separate random sample of 32 training pairs also contained modern passages with material from a preceding sentence. These observations identify both a generation failure and a limit on treating every paired book passage as a self-contained translation.
