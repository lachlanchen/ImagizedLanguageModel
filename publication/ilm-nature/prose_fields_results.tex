\subsection*{Learning whose value to copy from prose}
A human travel assistant recommended two restaurants in a single sentence and rated them 4.7 and 4.8. We kept the paragraph and question pixels fixed and changed the name of the restaurant in the prompt. The name-conditioned reader wrote the corresponding rating on both occasions (Fig.~\ref{fig:prose-fields}a). This was the sole native two-venue example passing our catalogue-identity and distinct-value screen in the official test dialogues.

Removing the printed catalogue rows required a new source locator. A 152,352-parameter interval reader fitted to 11,605 human utterances and 10,722 training assemblies located 1,079 of 1,087 marked property values exactly in 940 test utterances. It also proposed two spans for absent catalogue fields. These labels describe verbatim catalogue values. In the original dialogue a visitor could express the same information with different words. Pairing a human question with an eligible utterance from another test conversation about the same venue gave 181 single-property and three two-property questions. All 184 generated replies passed the literal attachment check; erasing the question left 87. Replacing a requested value with one from another held venue gave correct before-and-after replies on 181 of 184 pairs. Among twelve separately authored descriptions with competing or implicit facts, only three passed. One restaurant paragraph supplied the neighbour's 98-yuan price as the target's 61-yuan cost. Another reply inserted a judgment that a meal was inexpensive. Two catalogue values in a grammatical clause need not justify such a judgment.

The first locator receives the paragraph and domain. It must select the same interval when only the target name changes. We therefore supplied a new reader with the position of the target's printed name and trained on pages with two competing venues. Among 539 joined test comparisons, the name-conditioned optical reader and writer answered both names in both paragraph orders on 472. Training the same architecture with the name position erased gave zero; the matched byte input gave 487. A distance rule given the two true value locations completed 305. The observations are grouped combinations of reused human utterances. On twelve independently worded two-venue paragraphs the optical model answered both names on five and the byte model on four (Fig.~\ref{fig:prose-fields}b). The optical model gave the garden's admission price for the tower when the text referred to them as the former and latter. On the original single-property cases, correct attachments declined from 181 to 176 after name-conditioned fitting. For delayed-order prompts on 142 naturally occurring multi-property utterances, the preceding reader and writer passed neither direction together on any passage.

As a cross-corpus check, ten historic attraction-admission questions in DuReader supplied natural search queries and longer prose. A query-based excerpt selector preserved at least one compatible reference amount or free-admission statement on eight. The name-conditioned model returned a compatible core on six of ten, including one question with contradictory stored references. It gave the 35-yuan boat fee for an attraction with a 90-yuan starting ticket. Of two questions with a single designated fee, the model answered one. A single amount omits distinctions between seasons, concessions and advance prices. The image system contains 486,442 inference parameters. Its four required fits, including the earlier query reader and writer, took 57.9 seconds and 5.72 kJ of measured device energy on one RTX 4090 D. Data preparation, comparison networks, evaluation and host energy are excluded.
