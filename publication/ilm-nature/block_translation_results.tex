\paragraph{Translating from the classical source alone.}
In a deliberately confusable within-chapter sample, a blinded reader could identify the recorded assignment in 46 of 47 attempted pairs but judged both modern translations faithful in only 22 of 64 pairs. Several glosses described a neighboring event. An order-preserving realignment changed 75 of 55,872 eligible training pairs. Among 5,194 pairs from reserved chapters it changed two. Blind review preferred both replacements, although one remained partial. Among 24 unchanged controls, 23 were faithful. The difficult sample therefore cannot estimate the prevalence of misalignment in the archive.

We fitted two 1.70-million-parameter visual writers on identical pictured classical sources. The modern targets differed at 46 of the 32,768 training rows. At each source cell the writer chose how many images to produce and which to copy or draw. It received neither a modern prefix nor marked insertion sites during generation. On 2,048 passages the realigned writer made 36,980 glyph edits, compared with 36,984 for the original targets and 35,162 for direct copying (Fig.~\ref{fig:block-translation}). Reversing the viewed context while preserving the physical copy positions increased the realigned writer's error from 58.44\% to 61.86\%. Source order influenced its decisions, yet it frequently replaced content with the same common particle. Neither neural writer produced a fully acceptable modern gloss in a blinded reading of 24 passages. The recorded translations were accepted in 19.

A writer that composed observed bitmap phrases gave a different result. On 2,048 additional passages it made 32,909 edits over 61,342 target cells. The error rate was 53.65\%, compared with 56.62\% for copying and 55.84\% for local majority transfer. The chapter-bootstrap interval for the composer's reduction from copying was 2.53 to 3.39 percentage points. The composer exactly reproduced 55 entire glosses, including five requiring a change, all absent from training as complete glosses. Copying exactly reproduced 59, all unchanged. In the first changed exact answer, a one-glyph classical verb of death became its two-glyph modern equivalent while the date and proper name were preserved. In a different short passage the composer mishandled the same verb. Four of 24 composer outputs were judged acceptable, including an unchanged date, against two for local majority transfer, one for copying and 17 for the references. Ten composer outputs were erroneous. These paired readings came from one study-involved model reader. The source-only results support a limited ability to transfer visual phrases to new sentences. Reliable translation of unrestricted prose remains open.

Increasing the training corpus to 55,872 pictured pairs with the same phrase scoring rule produced 30 exact glosses on another 1,097 passages, including two changed answers absent from training. The smaller composer produced 27 and one, respectively. The error rate decreased from 52.44\% to 52.37\%, while copying gave 55.58\%. The chapter-bootstrap interval for large minus small was $-0.32$ to $+0.19$ percentage points. Four large-model outputs and three small-model outputs passed the 24-passage reading. The extra data gave little evidence of a consistent incremental benefit at this scale.
