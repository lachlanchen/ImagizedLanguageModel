\subsection*{Predicting missing words from neighbouring ink}
From the pictured words \emph{strong enough} and \emph{he could}, the visual writer supplied the missing word \emph{that} (Fig.~\ref{fig:context-cloze}). The four flanking words had never occurred together in its sampled training windows. Across 50,012 interior positions of 147 new passages the model recovered 4,863 literal word images (9.72\%). With neighbourhoods exchanged between stories it recovered 1,010 (2.02\%). The most frequent fitting word accounted for 2,537 (5.07\%). A frequency-count predictor that backed off to shorter contexts recovered 6,715 (13.43\%) using exactly the same sampled windows. The network's deficit was 3.70 percentage points (95\% paired story interval 3.34 to 4.07). The byte network recovered 4,924 words. Of all positions, 32,604 had a centre word inside the 2,048-image output memory.

Direct training on source-absent answer words also improved their ranking. Given the other answer words, the visual writer placed the correct stored word first on 16 of 115 positions, compared with five for its parent and 12 for the version trained on questions and answers alone. Mean reciprocal rank rose to 0.219 from 0.116 for the parent (difference 0.103, 95\% interval 0.045 to 0.163) and 0.052 for the fitting-frequency prior (difference 0.167, interval 0.111 to 0.227). Question-and-history exchange reduced the new score to 0.115. Passage exchange gave 0.199; the interval for that change included zero. Once copy and spelling competed with retrieval, only three of the 115 missing words were emitted correctly. Unassisted, the writer answered 484 of 2,234 questions exactly, falling to 32 after exchanging the question and history and 272 after exchanging the passage. The parent answered 479. Both writers matched the same one of 265 difficult references. The visual writer improved its conditional word ranking but failed the registered whole-answer criterion.

We also measured the cost of losing the original context during forward writing. With just two true preceding images, the network recovered 3,111 of 50,012 next words. Given two prefix images at a fixed location in each of the 147 passages, it produced only 36 distinct eight-word continuations. The first began after \emph{Nina, the} and read \emph{song of the song of the song of}. None matched all eight reference words. Supplying the true two-word history at every step recovered 70 of 1,176 positions, compared with 50 using self-generated histories. Two emitted words already reduced the 147 prompts to 36 distinct states. Even a perfect choice of one remaining suffix per reached state could therefore match at most 36 of the 147 human suffixes. The observed writer matched none of these six-word suffixes.
