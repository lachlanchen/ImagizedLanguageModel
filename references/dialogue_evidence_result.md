# Can supervision on source words improve visual reading?

A 547,125-parameter model can read aligned images of an English passage and question and write a source-dependent short reply. Highlighting which source words support the reply gave a small, uncertain improvement over ordinary training. Four paired fits shared the same pretrained visual parent. The two new groups of official CoQA development stories were absent from the original fitting and evaluation sets. All fits used one RTX 4090 D. ## What the ink answers show

The original evidence-supervised visual writer matched at least one human answer on 270 of the 758 turns. Exchanging the question and history reduced that to 29. Exchanging the passage reduced it to 74. Asked “Who took the tea first?”, the writer changed from *Black* to *Shen Nong* after evidence supervision. On the Cockney question it also changed a correct *Jane Humphreys* to *Madam*. Both answers stopped normally. ## What the mathematics predicts

Inside an annotated evidence interval let the pointer assign mass *a* to correct output words and *b* to incorrect ones. Let *c* and *d* be the corresponding masses outside. Increasing the weight of the interval by any integer factor greater than one increases the total probability of the correct word precisely when **ad > bc**. This compares correct-word enrichment inside and outside and accounts for repeated copies. Lean checks the equivalence by cross multiplication of nonnegative integer masses. On 1,834 eligible word positions, direct double-precision evaluation agreed within 5.1 × 10⁻¹⁵. Giving the parent the human interval and multiplying its weight by four corrected 135 pointer choices while spoiling ten. This measurement supplied the previous correct words. Replacing the human interval by the model's own ordered endpoint estimate produced uncertain F1 changes in both fresh groups. For matched-quality efficiency, Lean computes the cheapest recorded qualifying checkpoint in each representation. It proves that each minimum is attainable and that a factor advantage exists exactly when a qualifying visual checkpoint beats every qualifying token checkpoint by that factor. A reconstruction from the released accuracy counts and millijoules yields 5,195.723 J for images and 4,858.209 J for bytes at 20% exact on both held sets. These are recorded GPU preparation and training costs. Host energy, evaluation and unobserved intermediate checkpoints are outside this comparison. At 25% the visual curve has no qualifying checkpoint. The same corpus rendered losslessly in two ways preserves attainable prediction risk. A computational advantage requires a learning algorithm and data distribution that exploit a useful difference. ## Training signals and next experiment

The local Qwen3-4B teacher produced 633 and 660 responses across two qualification runs. On 83 questions requiring new phrasing, moving the current question after the previous exchange raised F1 from 21.1% to 27.6%. The revised prompt obtained 32.0% on nine independent difficult turns, below the required 35%. All student labels came from human CoQA annotations. The local teacher was offline throughout student training and deployment. [First qualification](evidence/dialogue_teacher_v1/qualification.json) · [Revised prompt and replication](evidence/dialogue_teacher_v1/current_last/qualification.json)

The next learning target is choosing and ordering visible words to compose an answer. Separate training questions whose answer requires reordering from those that additionally require spelling. Compare their learned replies against ordinary mixed training on unrehearsed stories. Require complete autonomous answers that respond to changes in both the question and the passage. A faithful historical-forms writer and a cheaper general language learner remain longer-term research objectives.

## A sufficient condition for a visual sample advantage

Consider two different written prompts that ask for the same answer. If an image encoder gives them the same key, and that key preserves the correct complete answer throughout its class, one correctly labelled example in that class suffices for an explicit learner to answer both. Lean proves this for arbitrary prompt images, response types and finite training lists. It also proves an identifiability limit for a learner observing only opaque IDs: exchanging the unknown meanings of two unseen IDs can leave all its training observations identical while changing the required test answer. The result gives a reason to seek context-sensitive, reusable visual features. It assumes that the key is available and semantically reliable. Learning its encoder incurs costs, real token embeddings can share features, and the same glyph can have different meanings in different sentences. The theorem yields neither a numerical energy advantage nor a general separation from token models. [Constructive learner and ambiguity proof](../formal/span_list/VisualClassTransfer.lean)
