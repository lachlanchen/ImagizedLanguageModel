# Reading word order and writing a translation from glyphs

A bidirectional visual writer learned to discriminate word order more reliably than it learned to produce words. On the new four-book panel the masked writer conveyed one complete meaning in 192 requests, the displaced writer zero, the causal parent one and quantized Qwen3-8B 169. The sole credited masking-only output translated *This was something new* as *这东西是新的。* A reference-hidden local model judged the four-book results in two candidate orders and credited 128/192 original references. The model-based scores have uncertainty, particularly for loosely aligned translations. Both new writers began from the same independent bilingual parent. Each completed four additional epochs over 268,342 paired passages in both directions, with 2,146,736 source presentations and 19,684,647 matched selected target positions. Their 3,252,192 trainable parameters include a source-only length predictor. Six confidence-based passes write a variable-length canvas from blank. The deployment uses a shared frozen physical glyph motor and finite strip inventory. The two fits took 288.9 and 293.0 seconds with under 1.56 GiB allocated on a single 4090 D. Training loss per selected strip fell to 5.82 and 4.72 nats respectively. A displaced canvas sometimes reveals part of the correct inventory, which makes training loss a poor estimate of free quality. The displaced-minus-masked four-book interval was −1.56 to 0.00 percentage points. The primary improvement criterion failed. All 3,136 free book, OPUS and controlled PNGs and terminations replayed on CPU. On 192 inputs the median elapsed times for single-request writing were 5.83, 2.19 and 2.02 seconds for parent, mask and displacement. In batches of sixteen they were 1.22, 0.140 and 0.091 seconds. These measurements include collecting the output ink. They exclude model loading and PNG compression. The fastest parallel writer failed every book meaning, so its throughput advantage cannot establish savings at useful language quality. ## Supplying content changes the question

With the actual unordered strips supplied, maximum-weight assignment from a blank canvas recovered 128/336 complete controlled answers under the masked writer. Offering that writer only the two correct alternative orders yielded 167/168 correct choices, with both sources correct in 83/84 equal-inventory reversal pairs. On this balanced panel the choices retain at least 0.947 bits of orientation information, conditional on the alternatives and pair identity. A separate deployment replay reversed candidate presentation and preserved all choices. [Reader and command](../models/visual_strip_restoration/README.md) · [Per-condition results](evidence/visual_strip_restoration_v1/supplied.json) · [Information bound](strip_orientation_information.md) · [Lean collision proof](../formal/span_list/VisualStripRecovery.lean)

That score concealed a simple explanation. On affirmative English actives the supplied-choice reader got 84/84 right. On equivalent passives, which reverse the order of the surface names relative to the agent, it got 0/84. It followed name order throughout. Separately, given partly obscured book targets, masked training recovered 60/962 missing strips with the correct source and 33/962 with exchanged sources. OPUS recovery rose from 21/918 to 85/918. The source image affects content, though most missing strips are still wrong. The subsequent marked and open participant readers directly tested this grammatical failure on authentic clauses.
