# Who surrounded whom?

The input picture shows the bronze name of Qin, the printed verb 围, the bronze name of Chu and a full stop. The writer answers with a picture that reads “Qin surrounded Chu.” Swap the two historical images and it writes “Chu surrounded Qin.” Change the verb to 救 and it repeats the rescuer. All four source–answer examples in Figure 63 are complete model emissions. The name strokes are the supplied 32 × 32 bitmaps. The surrounding modern writing and the decision to stop come from the writer. The scanned archive comprised 7,304 chapters. The parent had already trained on 55,872 paired passages. The targeted experiment used 203 real classical–modern event clauses from 132 chapters, each with two matched exposures per pass. Before generation we removed two invalid name extractions from the principal test. The resulting 40 original clauses each supplied a second, counterfactual participant order. Only 20 had historical images available for both states under the fixed selection rule. The broad test covered 305 different held-out chapters. The exchange–replay difference on broad edits was small, with a paired chapter-bootstrap interval spanning zero. One study-involved model reader graded shuffled alternatives from complete, aligned glyph identities. The small meaning samples and shared constructions limit the conclusions. ## Why the visual mapping helps

A name can change while the grammatical relation stays fixed. The model learns where this happens from matched pictured inputs and targets. At inference a table of visible neighbours proposes a familiar stand-in for each unfamiliar participant image, reserving distinct images absent elsewhere in the prompt. Each exchange is reversible. The writer emits the original physical image when its internal choice is the stand-in, then reads back what it emitted. This gives the generator a way to preserve previously unseen ink and still use a familiar construction. In 40 controls we put an unfamiliar historical image in the predicate position. It triggered zero participant assignments for that image. Glossing an unfamiliar word or recovering the identity of an unlabelled inscription requires further evidence. A frequency-only assignment achieved the same sampled whole-meaning result as contextual selection. Symbol permutation has a precedent in compositional language modelling, and autoregressive generation of text pixels also predates this work. [Gordon et al.](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=SylVNerFvr) · [Tai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03321)

The 2,251,925-parameter writer used sixty optimizer steps per arm, twelve passes and 3,914 target cells per pass. The exchange and replay fits took 1.40 s and 1.92 s on one RTX 4090 D, respectively. These are incremental fits on a shared trained parent. They exclude parent training, data preparation and evaluation. Whole-system efficiency at matched language quality remains unknown. ## Pixel verification

The historical cache uses one for paper and the writer uses one for ink. The initial export reversed foreground and background. We rendered all ten selected SVGs, authenticated their source and raster hashes, complemented the masks and repeated inference with identical weights. A preliminary corrected export applied a byte-level emptiness test that changed eight exemplars. We retained it separately and required the original hashes for the reported comparison. With matched exemplars the contextual writer changed from 12/20 to 14/20 ordered, rewritten pairs, the unwrapped writer from 1/20 to 4/20. After identifying corresponding cells, 32/40 contextual outputs were identical across polarities and all ten controlled Qin–Chu outputs were equivalent. Seventy-two repeated masked choices retained their original grades and 33 new alternatives were read under hidden method labels. Four displayed answers also reproduced byte for byte via the public CPU inference command. The next question is whether the writer can use surrounding language to choose between two meanings of the **same visible predicate**. Hold its bitmap and both participants fixed while changing contextual evidence. Evaluate complete free-written modern responses on unseen chapters, include shuffled-context and copying controls, and retain the diverse translation test. This directly tests the gap left by successful name transport and failed whole-passage translation.
